# The Jōban coalfield and the *sakiyama*

This is an English digest of **[docs/HISTORY.md](HISTORY.md)**, the research the game is built
on. The Japanese original is longer and carries the full citations, each graded **confirmed**
(primary source, museum exhibit or press report), **likely** (several sources agree, original
not seen) or **unverified**. Where this digest states a fact, the grade is given in the same way.

The game is a tribute. The man it is for is at the end of this page.

---

## The field

The Jōban coalfield lies on the Pacific coast of Japan, around Iwaki in Fukushima prefecture
and the north of Ibaraki. It was not a good coalfield. The coal was low-grade and heavy with
sulphur, and the strata were so sharply folded that following a seam meant digging deeper
rather than along. Closer to the sea the seams tilt down at 7–10 degrees, so the further the
work went seaward, the deeper and harder it became — in the end only the large companies
could work it at all (**likely**).

What made Jōban unlike other fields was the water. Ground water poured in, and so did hot
spring water; the pit was hot, and some of the largest drainage pumps in the world at the time
stood in rows to keep it down. In places **40 tonnes of water had to be lifted for every tonne
of coal** — the figure varies by pit and depth, which is why older sources quote four tonnes
and others forty (**confirmed**, Iwaki City Coal and Fossil Museum "Horuru").

Around **130 mines** once worked in and around Iwaki. Output peaked in the 1950s at roughly
**4 million tonnes a year**, close enough to Tokyo that it fed the factories of the capital
region and the postwar rebuilding (**confirmed**).

## What a *sakiyama* was

The word has two meanings, and the early Shōwa era (the 1930s–40s) sits in the change from one
to the other.

**As one half of a working pair.** At the face, the one who cut the coal was the *sakiyama*;
the one who carried it out was the *atoyama*. One *sakiyama* and one or two *atoyama* formed a
single unit, and the *atoyama* worked under the *sakiyama*'s direction. The pair was often
husband and wife, or father and daughter. At Jōban women worked underground as haulers,
hewers and timbermen, and above ground at the picking tables (**confirmed**).

**As the skilled man who ran the place.** Later the *sakiyama* was the experienced worker and
the *atoyama* the apprentice: one *sakiyama* to two or three *atoyama* at a working, with the
*sakiyama* directing. A particularly accomplished one was an **ō-sakiyama** (great *sakiyama*),
and one who ran a longwall face was called a **"long-chō"** (**confirmed**).

The protagonist of this game is a *sakiyama* in the second sense.

## The heat, the water, and the cold bath

A photographer who went down in the 1950s recorded **40 °C and 98 % humidity**. Coming from a
cool roadway into the working, his camera fogged as if it had been dipped in water. The men
cut coal in loincloths. Hewers **worked with salt in their mouths** to keep from collapsing
(**likely**).

And there was a **cold bath in the pit**. The museum at Iwaki has a reconstructed roadway with
an exhibit of miners sitting in it. By the curator's account the cycle was:

> work 15 to 30 minutes → get into the cold bath → eat a *umeboshi* (a salted plum) to put the
> salt back → go again.

(**confirmed**, Yomiuri Shimbun Fukushima edition, 30 April 2026, and the museum exhibit.)

**This is what the game's body-heat meter is made of.** The meter fills while you dig; the cold
bath on the board cools you while you sit in it, and you can use it as often as you like.
A rule limiting it to once per stage was rejected because the history says the opposite: the
bath was used over and over. The family of the author had passed down a story about a bath in
the pit, and the newspaper and the museum turned out to agree with it.

## What killed people

A list of accidents compiled from the Iwaki city history divides the danger into four kinds.
Counted by type, **gas came first**, then flooding, then heat, then roof falls.

| Kind | Note | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gas explosion** (most frequent) | The cause column repeats "smoking", "the lamp flame", "shot firing" | Aug 1924, Iriyama No. 5 — 75 dead; May 1935, Iriyama No. 6 — 49 dead |
| **Inrush of water** | Common at Jōban in particular; the other face of all that water | 1905, Ibaraki Anthracite — 8 dead, 28 overcome; 1938, Hōjō — 9 dead |
| **Underground fire** | The worst single toll | Mar 1927, Machida shaft, Iwaki Tankō — 134 dead (Wikipedia gives 131 including the rescue party; **the figures differ**) |
| **Roof fall / haulage** | Many incidents, few deaths each | 1955, Kashima — 6 dead; 1922, a man-car rope parted — 16 dead |

During the war it got worse: deaths from industrial accidents in the field went 88 (1939) →
116 (1940) → 131 (1944) → 176 (1945), close to 800 in total. Korean labourers, brought in under
wartime mobilisation, are among them (*Iwaki City History, separate volume: A History of the
Jōban Coalfield*, pp. 508, 511).

## Pneumoconiosis, and the lawsuit that came first

Coal dust settles in the lungs and they stiffen. Pneumoconiosis is **irreversible, it goes on
progressing after the dust work stops, and there is still no treatment for the disease itself**;
it invites pneumonia and other infections, and can bring on lung cancer. The official
classification of a case often comes years after retirement — **it shows itself after the
working life, not during it** (**confirmed**, Japanese government sources).

On **24 January 1992** the appeal in the first Jōban pneumoconiosis suit was settled: 83 former
miners of the old Jōban mines and bereaved families, who had sued for ¥746 million, settled for
¥420 million paid by the company, about six years and four months after filing. **It was the
first settlement of a large class action of its kind in Japan** (**confirmed**, Fukushima Minpō).

The sequence matters. Jōban's settlement against the company came in 1992; the Supreme Court
held the state liable for failing to exercise its regulatory powers twelve years later, in the
Chikuhō case of 2004. The first time the harm of coal work was recognised in law, it happened
on this field.

**None of this is in the game as a mechanic, by the author's decision.** Overheating is in,
because it happened in the pit. Pneumoconiosis comes after a man leaves the pit, and making it
a losing condition would not fit the subject. It is recorded here instead, which is where it
belongs. *This document is not legal advice.*

## The other half of it

The research was deliberately not allowed to lean only dark.

- The pits ran 24 hours on three shifts, so the drinking places near the gates **were busy at
  every hour of the day**.
- The work was dangerous and **the pay was good**; households were comfortable.
- There were theatres. "Every entertainer except Misora Hibari came through," people said.

(**confirmed**, Yomiuri Shimbun Fukushima edition, 30 April 2026.)

This is why the title tune is *Down in a Coal Mine* and the in-game tune is *Jōban Tankō-bushi*,
the local miners' folk song. The field was a hard place to work and a lively place to live, and
the game is allowed to be both.

## The man

> The author's grandfather is named here **with the permission of his family** (his only
> daughter, the author's mother, 21 October 2026). The name is kept to the research documents;
> in the README and in the game itself the character is **"Tanno 3Ji"**, which is not his name.

**Tanno Sanji** was a *sakiyama* at the Jōban coal mine. He died without ever talking about
what the pit was like. The game exists because his grandson wanted to gather what was hard,
what was punishing and what was good about that work into something that could be handed on.

He loved to drink. When he married, his wife asked him for one thing: **not to drink his way
around the town**. He agreed. In exchange she promised him her cooking every day, and good
food — sashimi on the table. **Both of them kept that bargain for the rest of their lives.**

The mining town around him ran all night, the bars by the pit gates were full, and the money
was there. Everything was in place for a man to drink his way around the town. **He was the one
who walked straight home.**

So the verb the game ends on is not "conquer" or "dig it all out". It is **go home**. The
ending is a shift finishing and a man walking back to the row house where his wife and daughter
are waiting, with the food and the sake he liked. The character never speaks, the whole way
through — he was a quiet man, and the quiet was not resignation but the same thing that kept
the bargain: deciding something and then holding to it.

The dedication at the end of the credit roll reads:

> TANNO 3JI IS A NAME I MADE UP
> FOR A MAN WHO REALLY LIVED.
> THIS GAME IS FOR HIM,
> FOR EVERY SAKIYAMA,
> AND FOR THE FAMILIES WHO WAITED.

pathfinder, the memoirs, and the haiku and tanka collections written by the miners themselves,

(tankouisan.jp), the **Iwaki City Coal and Fossil Museum "Horuru"** with its reconstructed
